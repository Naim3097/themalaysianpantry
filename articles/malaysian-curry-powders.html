Meat, fish or chicken: why curry powders differ

Three blends at the same price, and using the wrong one is the usual reason a fish curry tastes oddly heavy.

Section
Ingredients
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
8 min

Walk down the spice aisle of any Malaysian supermarket and you will find curry powders labelled for meat, for fish, and often for chicken, sitting beside each other at the same price. A great many households buy one and use it for everything.

They are genuinely different blends, formulated for different jobs, and using the wrong one is the most common reason a fish curry tastes oddly heavy.

Meat curry powder

Built around warmth and depth. Coriander leads, usually by a considerable margin, with cumin and fennel behind it, then the warm aromatics: cinnamon, star anise, cloves, cardamom. Turmeric is present but not dominant.

This blend is designed for long cooking with robust proteins. Beef, mutton and chicken all stand up to it, and the warm spices need time to soften and merge. Used in a quick dish they can taste raw and medicinal.

Fish curry powder

A different balance entirely. More turmeric, giving that yellower colour. More chilli. Less of the warm sweet spice, and crucially, fenugreek, which is the ingredient that most distinguishes it.

Fenugreek is faintly bitter and slightly maple-like, and it does something specific with seafood: it cuts through and complements fishiness in a way the warm spices do not. It is also why fish curry powder smells noticeably different from meat curry powder in the packet.

Fish cooks in minutes rather than hours, so the blend has to deliver its flavour fast, and it has to avoid the heavy notes that would bury a delicate protein.

Why it matters

Fish curries in Malaysia are usually sour as well as spiced, most often with asam keping. That combination of clean acidity, turmeric, chilli and fenugreek is what asam pedas tastes like.

Swap in meat curry powder and you get cinnamon and star anise fighting the sourness. The dish is not ruined, but it stops being the dish you meant to make.

Chicken curry powder

Where it exists as a separate product, it usually sits between the two: warmer than fish, lighter than meat, often with more turmeric than a meat blend.

Chicken is forgiving and works with either of the other two, so this is the least essential of the three to own. If you are buying only two, buy meat and fish.

Freshness matters more than brand

Ground spices lose their volatile aromatics steadily once ground, and a curry powder that has sat open in a warm kitchen for a year is substantially weaker than the same product bought fresh.

The practical consequence: buy small packets more often rather than a large tin annually. A cheaper powder bought fresh will usually outperform an expensive one that has been open for eighteen months.

Smell it. A good curry powder should be immediately aromatic when you open the packet. If you have to put your nose in it to smell anything, it is past useful.

Bloom it in oil

This is the step that separates a good curry from a dusty one, and it takes ninety seconds.

Curry powder added directly to liquid tastes raw and slightly gritty. Fried briefly in oil first, ideally after the rempah and before the liquid goes in, the spices bloom: fat-soluble aromatic compounds dissolve into the oil and distribute through the entire dish rather than sitting in suspension.

Make it a paste with a splash of water first if you are worried about scorching, then fry that until it darkens slightly and smells toasted rather than dusty. It burns quickly, so this is not a step to walk away from.

Blending your own

Worth doing once, if only to understand what is in the packet. Toast whole spices lightly, cool them completely, then grind. The difference in aroma against a commercial powder is immediately obvious.

Whether it is worth doing regularly is a different question. Good commercial blends are formulated carefully and cost very little, and the honest answer for most households is that a fresh packet from a decent brand is the better use of your time. Grinding your own matters most if you want a specific profile that nothing on the shelf provides.

Storage

Airtight, dark, and not in the cupboard above the stove, which is where most people keep spices and is the worst place in the kitchen for them. Heat drives off aromatics faster than anything except time.

Six months for an opened packet is a reasonable working life. Longer than that and it still works, it is simply doing less than you think it is, which is why some cooks keep adding more and wondering why the curry stays flat.
