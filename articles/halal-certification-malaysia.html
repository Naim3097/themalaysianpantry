Halal, explained

How halal certification actually works in Malaysia

Most people assume it is a check on the recipe. It is mostly a check on everything around the recipe, which is why so many first applications come back with corrections.

7 September 2026 · 9 min read

Ask a small food producer what halal certification involves and most will describe an ingredient list. No pork, no alcohol, done. That instinct is understandable and it is also the reason a lot of first applications stall. The ingredients matter, but they are perhaps a third of what is actually being assessed. The rest is your premises, your process, your paperwork and your people.

This is a plain walk through what the process actually looks like in Malaysia, written for someone running a small or growing food business who wants to know what they are signing up for before they start.

Who issues it, and why that matters

The Malaysian halal certificate, the Sijil Halal Malaysia, is issued by JAKIM, the Department of Islamic Development Malaysia, working alongside the state religious authorities. There is no private route. Consultants can help you prepare, and plenty do, but no consultant issues the certificate.

This is worth understanding because Malaysia's certification carries unusual weight internationally. A JAKIM certificate opens doors in export markets in a way that a generic halal claim does not, which is a large part of why manufacturers here pursue it even when their domestic customers are not asking.

The standard you are being measured against

For food and beverage, the reference document is MS 1500:2019, the Malaysian halal standard. For non-food consumer goods there is MS 2400. If you take one action after reading this, make it obtaining a copy of MS 1500 and reading the sections that apply to your operation. Almost nobody does this before applying, and it is the single cheapest way to avoid a round of corrections.

The standard covers sourcing, processing, hygiene, premises and handling. Note what that list implies: a fully halal ingredient deck processed in a facility that cannot demonstrate separation and cleanliness will not pass.

Applying

Applications go through MYeHALAL, JAKIM's online system. You register the company, complete the form, upload documents and pay a fee that scales with company size, broadly in the range of a few hundred ringgit. The fee is not the expensive part. The preparation is.

What you will need to hand

  • SSM company registration
  • Premises licence from your local authority
  • A complete ingredient list, with halal evidence for every single ingredient
  • Your manufacturing process, documented as a flow
  • Staff details, including the Muslim staff involved in production

The third item is where most of the work sits, and where most applications come unstuck.

Why the ingredient list is harder than it looks

"Halal evidence for every ingredient" sounds procedural until you try it. You are not being asked whether an ingredient is halal in principle. You are being asked to produce documentation from your supplier proving the specific material you bought, from the specific plant it came from, is certified.

For a seasoning blend with twenty inputs, that is twenty supplier certificates, each current, each matching the material actually in your store. If one supplier has let a certificate lapse, or supplies from a different plant than the one on the certificate, that is a gap you have to close before the audit.

This is also where small producers discover that a cheaper supplier without documentation is not actually cheaper. An ingredient you cannot evidence is an ingredient you cannot use, whatever it costs.

The audit

An audit team visits. What they look at is broader than most first-timers expect:

  • Raw material storage, and whether halal and non-halal materials are genuinely separated
  • The production process and the equipment it runs on
  • Cleanliness and hygiene practice, as practised rather than as documented
  • Record keeping, including traceability from incoming material to finished batch
  • Staff competency and training
  • Whether your halal assurance system exists in reality or only on paper

That last point deserves emphasis. Auditors have seen a great many binders. What distinguishes a strong audit from a weak one is whether the person on the line can explain the procedure without reading it.

Where applications actually fail

Three things account for most corrections.

Shared equipment

If a line handles both certified and uncertified products, you need a defensible separation regime, and in some cases dedicated equipment. Producers often assume a thorough wash covers it. Depending on what was previously run, it may not.

Cleaning that does not meet the requirement

Where equipment has been in contact with material considered najs, ordinary cleaning is not sufficient. Ritual cleansing, samak, is required, and it has a specific method. This surprises people who have run spotless kitchens for years.

Documentation that lags reality

Formulations change. Suppliers change. The certified formulation on file and the one actually being produced drift apart, quietly, over months. Auditors find this quickly because they compare the batch record with the certificate.

How long, and then what

Plan in months rather than weeks. The variable is rarely JAKIM's processing time; it is how long your own preparation takes, particularly chasing supplier documentation from companies that are in no hurry.

Certificates run for two years, and renewal should be filed at least three months before expiry. Treat that as a hard date. A lapsed certificate is not a paperwork inconvenience if a customer's own audit falls in the same window.

If you are buying rather than making

There is a distinction worth knowing, because it causes genuine confusion: a halal certified ingredient and a halal certified facility are different claims. Buying certified ingredients does not make your finished product certified. Conversely, a certified facility still has to evidence every ingredient going into it.

For smaller brands, this is often the argument for manufacturing through an established certified producer rather than certifying a facility of your own. Contract manufacturers who already hold certification, such as Tropicor Foods, carry the facility side of the requirement, which removes the largest fixed cost from a first product launch. It is not the right answer for everyone, but it is worth pricing before committing to your own plant.

Is it worth it

If you sell to Muslim consumers in Malaysia, the question mostly answers itself. Beyond that, the honest calculation is about who you want to sell to next. Certification is what supermarket buyers, foodservice groups and export customers ask for early in a conversation, often before they ask about price. Businesses that get certified before they need it tend to find the opportunity arrives sooner.

If you are not there yet, the useful preparation costs nothing: start collecting halal documentation from every supplier now, and keep your formulation records current. Those two habits are most of the work, and they are far easier to build early than to reconstruct later.
