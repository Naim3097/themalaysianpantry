Technique

Sweet, salty, sour, spicy: how to fix a dish that tastes wrong

Experienced cooks diagnose this in seconds. It is not intuition, it is a short checklist run quickly.

Section
Technique
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
8 min

Ask an experienced cook why a dish tastes wrong and they will usually answer within a few seconds, without tasting it twice. That is not intuition so much as a short mental checklist, run quickly. This is the checklist.

Malaysian cooking works across four axes: masin salty, manis sweet, masam sour, pedas spicy. Sitting underneath all of them is savoury depth, which is not really a taste you balance so much as a floor everything else stands on.

The point is not that a dish must contain all of them. It is that whichever are present should hold each other in tension, so no single one arrives alone.

Where each one comes from

Salt arrives as salt, but far more often as soy sauce, fish sauce, belacan, or a stock. Each brings savoury depth with it, which is why seasoning a Malaysian dish with plain salt alone often leaves it tasting oddly bare.

Sweet is usually gula Melaka or kicap manis rather than white sugar, and both bring a faint smokiness and caramel that plain sugar does not. Coconut also reads as sweet even without added sugar.

Sour comes from tamarind, from asam keping, from lime, and sometimes from tomato. These are not interchangeable: tamarind is round and slightly sweet, lime is sharp and volatile, asam keping is cleaner and more austere.

Heat comes from fresh chilli, dried chilli, or a paste built from either. Dried chillies give colour and depth more than raw heat; fresh chillies give brightness and bite.

The diagnostic

When something tastes not-quite-right, the fault is almost always one of these six.

It tastes flat and boring

Salt first, in small increments. If more salt makes it saltier without making it better, the problem was never salt: add acid. A squeeze of lime rescues more dull curries than any other single move.

It is too salty

Acid or sweetness, not water. Water dilutes everything equally and leaves you with a weak dish that is still slightly too salty. A little sugar and a squeeze of lime will pull the salt back into line while keeping the intensity.

It is too sour

Sugar is the obvious answer and usually the right one. Fat is the underrated answer: a spoon of coconut milk or a little oil rounds sharpness in a way sugar cannot, without making the dish sweet.

It is too sweet

Acid, then salt. Sweetness is the hardest to reverse, which is the argument for adding gula Melaka late and in stages rather than early and confidently.

It is too spicy

Fat first: coconut milk, yoghurt, oil. Fat physically carries capsaicin away from your palate in a way water does not, which is also why drinking water after chilli achieves nothing. Sugar and acid both help as well.

It tastes thin, but nothing is missing

This is the savoury floor problem, and it is the one most home cooks misread as a salt problem. The dish needs depth rather than seasoning: belacan, dried shrimp, a yeast extract, fish sauce, or simply a rempah that was not fried long enough. If a curry tastes like a list of ingredients rather than one thing, look at how long the rempah was fried before reaching for anything else.

Two rules that prevent most of this

Build early, adjust late. Salt and aromatics go in early so they have time to work through the dish. Acid, fresh chilli and sugar go in near the end, where you can taste as you go and stop.

Taste at serving temperature. Cold and lukewarm food tastes less salty and less sweet than the same food hot. Season a curry when it is barely warm and you will overshoot.

The order to work in

When a dish needs fixing, adjust in this order and taste between each: savoury depth, then salt, then acid, then sweetness, then heat.

The reason for that order is that the earlier items change how you perceive the later ones. Adding acid to an under-seasoned dish tastes wrong; adding it to a properly seasoned one tastes like the missing piece. Fixing sweetness before salt means fixing it twice.

Why the last squeeze of lime works

A great many Malaysian dishes finish with lime at the table rather than in the pot, and that is not decoration.

The aromatic compounds in citrus are volatile and largely cooked away within a minute or two of heat. Lime added at the end of cooking contributes sourness; lime squeezed over the plate contributes sourness and aroma, and the aroma is most of what you notice.

The same logic applies to fresh herbs, sliced chilli and fried shallots. What goes on at the end is doing something different from what went in at the start, and the two are not substitutes.
