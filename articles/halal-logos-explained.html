Halal logos: what counts, and what only looks official

One official mark, and a great many things that resemble one. Almost none mean what shoppers assume.

Section
Halal
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
8 min

There is one official halal mark in Malaysia and a great many things that resemble one. Some of the lookalikes are honest attempts to communicate something true. Others are decoration. Almost none of them mean what shoppers assume they mean.

The official mark

The Malaysian halal certification mark is issued under JAKIM and the state religious authorities. It has a fixed design and it carries a certificate reference. It is not a generic crescent, not an Arabic word rendered decoratively, and not a green circle a designer produced in-house.

The important consequence: a genuine mark is verifiable. There is a certificate behind it, held by a named company, covering named products or premises, with an expiry date. Anything that cannot be traced to such a record is not certification, whatever it looks like.

"No pork, no lard" is not certification

This sign is everywhere in Malaysia, usually in restaurant windows, and it is the most widely misread thing in the whole category.

It is a factual statement made voluntarily by the business about two ingredients. It is not assessed by anyone, it involves no inspection, and it says nothing about alcohol in cooking, about the source of gelatine or enzymes, about how meat was slaughtered, or about whether utensils are shared.

Some businesses displaying it are entirely scrupulous and simply have not pursued certification, which is a real and legitimate position. Others use it precisely because it sounds official while committing to very little. The sign does not let you tell those apart.

It is a claim, not a certificate. Whether that is enough is a personal decision, but it should be made knowing which of the two it is.

Verify the number, not the picture

A logo can be copied in a minute. A certificate reference either exists in the official records or it does not.

JAKIM maintains a public Halal Malaysia directory where certificate holders can be looked up. If a mark matters to your decision, that is where the answer is, and it takes under a minute on a phone.

Foreign logos on imported products

This is where it gets genuinely complicated, and where confident advice is usually wrong.

Halal certification bodies exist all over the world, and they vary enormously in rigour. JAKIM publishes a list of foreign certification bodies it recognises, and that list is the operative fact for imported goods in Malaysia.

A product carrying a mark from a recognised body is on solid ground. A product carrying a mark from a body not on that list is not necessarily suspect, but it has not been assessed against the standard Malaysia applies, and it is not equivalent to a JAKIM certificate even though the packaging may look equally official.

Because the recognised list changes, the useful habit is to check the current one rather than to memorise names. For a business importing ingredients, this is not optional: an auditor will ask whether your supplier's certification is recognised, and the answer needs to be evidenced.

Premises and products are certified separately

A restaurant holding halal certification is certified as a premises: the kitchen, the handling, the supply chain, the staff practice.

A packaged product is certified as a product, made in a certified facility, from evidenced ingredients.

These are different scopes and they do not transfer. A certified restaurant does not make everything it sells a certified product, and a company holding certification for one product line has not thereby certified the others. Certificates name what they cover, which is another reason the document matters more than the badge.

Certification expires

Malaysian halal certificates run for a fixed period and must be renewed. A certificate displayed on a wall may have lapsed, and a lapsed certificate is not a current one.

For consumers this rarely matters much. For a business buying ingredients it matters a great deal, because your own audit will test whether the supplier certificates in your file were valid at the time you bought the material. A certificate that expired six months ago does not evidence anything about last month's delivery.

Keeping supplier certificates current is unglamorous administrative work, and it is most of what separates a smooth halal audit from a painful one. We covered what that audit actually involves in how halal certification actually works.

The short version

  • One official Malaysian mark, backed by a verifiable certificate
  • "No pork no lard" is a voluntary claim, not certification
  • Foreign marks depend on whether the body is recognised by JAKIM
  • Premises certification and product certification are different things
  • Certificates expire, and an expired one evidences nothing
