Kicap masin, pekat and manis: which soy sauce does what

Three bottles that are not interchangeable, and the label detail that separates a good one from a cheap one.

Section
Ingredients
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
7 min

Most Malaysian kitchens keep at least three bottles of soy sauce, and a fair number of cooks could not say precisely what separates them beyond one being darker. They are genuinely different products with different jobs, and swapping one for another is why a dish sometimes comes out too salty, too sweet, or the wrong colour entirely.

Kicap masin

Light soy, the salty one. Thin, translucent brown, and the workhorse. This is your seasoning soy: it goes into stir-fries, marinades, dipping sauces and anywhere you want savouriness without changing the colour of the dish much.

If a recipe says only "soy sauce" with no further description, this is almost always what it means.

It is saltier than the dark varieties, which surprises people who assume darker means stronger. Colour and salt run in opposite directions here.

Kicap pekat

Dark or thick soy. Aged longer, often with molasses added, noticeably viscous and considerably less salty than light soy.

Its main job is colour and a faint bitter-sweet depth. A teaspoon turns a pale noodle dish that appetising mahogany brown. Used alone it will not season a dish properly, which is why most recipes use both: light for the salt, dark for the colour.

Because it is thick, it clings. Add it to the wok rather than at the table.

Kicap manis

The sweet one, and the most distinctly regional of the three. Thickened and sweetened, usually with palm sugar, into something closer to a syrup than a sauce.

This is what makes satay sauce taste like satay sauce, and what goes over nasi goreng, into marinades for grilled chicken, and across fried noodles. It caramelises fast, which is both its great virtue and the reason it burns if you add it too early over high heat.

Brands vary enormously in sweetness. Taste a new bottle before assuming it behaves like the last one.

Which one, quickly

  • Seasoning anything — kicap masin
  • Colour in noodles or braises — kicap pekat, a small amount
  • Marinades, satay, anything grilled or glazed — kicap manis
  • Most stir-fries — light for salt plus a little dark for colour

Brewed against the fast version

This is the distinction worth understanding, because it separates a good bottle from a cheap one and it is visible on the label if you know what you are looking at.

Traditionally brewed soy sauce is fermented over months. Soybeans and wheat are inoculated with mould, brined, and left. That slow fermentation produces hundreds of aromatic compounds alongside the salt and glutamates, which is where the complexity comes from.

The industrial shortcut breaks soy protein down chemically in hours rather than months, then adds colour, salt and flavourings to approximate the result. It is much cheaper and it tastes flatter, sharper and more one-dimensional.

The label usually tells you. Look for a short ingredient list: soybeans, wheat, salt, water, and little else. Long lists with hydrolysed vegetable protein, caramel colouring and multiple flavour enhancers point the other way.

This is one of the few places where paying twice as much genuinely tastes twice as good, particularly in dipping sauces where the soy is not hidden behind anything.

A note on halal status

Soy sauce comes up constantly in halal questions, and the answer is more nuanced than either camp usually admits.

Traditional brewing produces small amounts of alcohol as a natural by-product of fermentation, in the same way bread does. That is a different matter from alcohol added as an ingredient, and certification bodies assess it against published thresholds rather than banning fermentation outright.

The practical answer is the same as it is for most processed ingredients: check for certification rather than reasoning from the process. We went through why the label alone cannot settle these questions in the grey areas.

Storage

Soy sauce does not spoil in any dangerous sense; the salt sees to that. What it does is oxidise, going gradually darker, flatter and harsher over months in a warm cupboard.

Refrigerating after opening genuinely preserves the flavour, and it matters most for the good brewed bottles you paid extra for. For cooking soy that disappears within a month, the cupboard is fine.

Kicap manis is the exception worth watching. Its sugar content means it thickens and can crystallise at the neck. Wipe the rim, keep the cap clean, and it will pour properly a year later.
