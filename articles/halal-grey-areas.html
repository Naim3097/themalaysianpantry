Halal, explained

The grey areas: emulsifiers, enzymes and alcohol in flavourings

Some ingredients genuinely cannot be judged from the label. Not because the label is hiding something, but because the same name covers materials from completely different sources.

Section
Halal
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
9 min

There is a large amount of confident, wrong information circulating about halal ingredients, much of it in forwarded messages listing E numbers to avoid. Some of those lists have been passed around for twenty years and were inaccurate when they were written.

The genuine difficulty is narrower and more interesting than the lists suggest. A handful of ingredient categories are ambiguous by nature, because the same declared name can describe materials made from plants, from microbes, or from animals. This is a look at which ones, and what to do about it.

One thing to say at the outset: this is background, not a ruling. Specific determinations are made by certification bodies against published standards, and where a decision matters, that is where it should come from.

Emulsifiers, and E471 in particular

Emulsifiers hold fat and water together. Without them a great deal of processed food would separate on the shelf.

The one that comes up constantly is E471, mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids. Those fatty acids can come from vegetable oils, most commonly palm, or from animal fat. The declared name is identical either way.

So E471 is not inherently anything. A product containing it may be entirely plant-derived or not, and no amount of staring at the ingredient panel resolves it. The same logic applies to several related emulsifiers, including E472 and its variants.

This is the root of the confusion behind most of those forwarded lists. Someone discovered E471 can be animal-derived, and the message became "E471 is haram", which is not what the original finding said.

Enzymes

Enzymes are used across food production: in cheese, in baking, in juice clarification, in protein processing. They can be produced by microbial fermentation, extracted from plants, or taken from animal tissue.

Rennet is the familiar example. Traditional rennet comes from the stomach lining of a calf. Microbial and fermentation-produced rennet is now widespread and does not. Both may be declared simply as rennet, or folded into a generic term.

Enzymes are also frequently used as processing aids rather than ingredients, which in some labelling regimes means they need not appear on the panel at all. That is not concealment; it is a labelling convention. But it does mean the ingredient list is not a complete account of what touched the product.

Alcohol in flavourings

This one causes more argument than the rest combined, and much of the argument comes from two different things sharing a word.

Ethanol is widely used as a solvent and carrier in flavour extracts. Vanilla extract is the obvious case: the flavour compounds are extracted into alcohol because that is what dissolves them. The alcohol is a vehicle, present in tiny quantities in the finished food, and usually largely driven off during processing.

That is a different question from intoxicating drink. Certification standards distinguish between the two, and set limits on residual alcohol in finished products rather than banning the solvent outright. The specifics vary between certifying bodies, which is precisely why this is a matter for the certifier and not for a rule of thumb.

What it means practically: seeing "natural flavouring" on a label tells you very little on its own, and the presence of an alcohol-extracted flavour does not automatically place a product in one category or the other.

The ingredients that actually need checking

  • Mono- and diglycerides, E471 and relatives
  • Gelatine, which may be bovine, porcine or fish
  • Enzymes, including rennet
  • L-cysteine, a dough conditioner with several possible sources
  • Natural and artificial flavourings, where the carrier is not declared
  • Glycerine and glycerol
  • Stearates and stearic acid

None of these is inherently one thing. All of them require knowing the source.

Why the label cannot answer this

Food labelling exists mainly to inform consumers about allergens, nutrition and broad composition. It was never designed to trace the origin of every processing input, and it does not.

The result is that two products with identical ingredient panels can have genuinely different halal status, depending on suppliers neither panel mentions. This is not a flaw anyone is hiding. It is simply the limit of what a label does.

Which leads to the only reliable method.

Check the certificate, not the ingredient

A halal certificate from a recognised body means somebody has already done the work: traced every ingredient to its source, obtained documentation from each supplier, and inspected the facility. That is a body of evidence no consumer can reconstruct from a packet.

For a business buying ingredients, the same principle applies more strictly. You are not assessing whether an ingredient sounds acceptable. You are collecting a current certificate for the specific material from the specific supplier, and this is exactly what an auditor will ask to see. We covered what that looks like in practice in how halal certification actually works.

A note on imported products

Foreign halal logos vary enormously in what stands behind them. Some foreign certifiers are recognised by JAKIM; many are not, and some marks are effectively self-declared.

An imported product bearing an unfamiliar halal mark is not necessarily a problem, but it is a genuinely different level of assurance from a JAKIM certificate, and worth knowing the difference rather than treating every logo as equivalent.

What to take from this

The forwarded lists of forbidden E numbers are mostly noise. The real situation is narrower: a specific set of ingredient categories where the source cannot be determined from the name, and where certification is the only practical answer.

For a household, buying certified products handles it. For a business, it means the documentation habit matters more than ingredient knowledge, and it is worth building early. Chasing supplier certificates is far easier as a routine than as a scramble three weeks before an audit.
