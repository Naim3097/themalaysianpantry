Santan: fresh, boxed, powder or canned

Four versions of the same ingredient, sitting metres apart in the supermarket, and they do not behave the same way.

Section
Ingredients
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
8 min

Four versions of the same ingredient sit within a few metres of each other in any Malaysian supermarket, and they do not behave the same way. Fresh santan from the wet market, UHT in a box, powder in a sachet, and thick cream in a can. Recipes rarely say which they mean.

They are not interchangeable, and using the wrong one is behind a lot of curries that taste thin and a lot of desserts that taste of nothing much.

Fresh

Pressed from grated coconut, usually that morning. At the wet market you will often be offered two grades: pekat, the thick first press, and cair, the thinner second press made by adding water to the same flesh and pressing again.

Traditional cooking uses both, and uses them at different moments. The thin goes in early and simmers with the dish. The thick goes in near the end, where it will not be boiled hard enough to break.

Fresh is unquestionably the best tasting. It is also the most difficult: it sours within a day even refrigerated, it separates readily, and it will not wait for your schedule. If you buy it in the morning, cook with it that day.

UHT in a box

The sensible default for most cooking, and what most Malaysian households now use on a weekday. It is consistent, it keeps unopened for months, and it behaves predictably in a pan.

What to look at on the box is the fat content, which varies more than people expect between brands. Higher fat gives you a richer curry and holds up better to heat. Many boxes also contain stabilisers, which is not a defect: it is precisely what stops the thing splitting when you get distracted.

The trade is flavour. Boxed santan tastes cleaner and flatter than fresh. In a heavily spiced curry you will barely notice. In something delicate, you will.

Powder

Coconut milk dried onto a carrier. It keeps almost indefinitely, takes no fridge space, and lets you mix exactly the thickness you want, which is genuinely useful.

It is also the flattest tasting of the four, and it can turn gritty if you dump it into hot liquid. Mix it with warm water into a smooth paste first, then add. Treat it as a practical option for stocks, gravies and anywhere santan is a supporting note rather than the main event.

Canned cream

Thicker and higher in fat than boxed, and the closest thing to fresh thick santan available off a shelf. This is what you want for desserts, for rendang, and for anywhere the coconut is the point.

Cans often separate in storage, with solid cream at the top and thin liquid beneath. That is normal, not spoilage. Whether you shake the can or spoon off the top depends on what you are making; for a rich dessert, the solid part alone is often exactly right.

The short version

  • Everyday curry — boxed UHT, higher fat if there is a choice
  • Rendang and desserts — canned cream, or fresh thick if you have it
  • Something delicate — fresh, cooked the same day
  • Stocks and gravies — powder is fine and nobody will know

Why it splits, and when that is fine

Coconut milk is an emulsion: fat suspended in water, held together loosely. Heat, acid and time all work against that suspension, and when it fails the fat separates out and the sauce looks curdled.

Three things cause it more than anything else. Boiling hard rather than simmering. Adding acid, tamarind or lime, while the pan is at a rolling boil. And leaving it alone; santan wants occasional stirring, which keeps the fat moving rather than pooling.

The practical rules are simple. Add thick santan late. Keep the heat at a bare simmer once it is in. Stir now and then. Add acid off the heat, or at least off the boil.

But splitting is not always a fault. In rendang it is the entire point: you cook the santan down deliberately until the oil separates and the meat begins to fry in it, which is what makes rendang rendang rather than a wet curry. The same event is a mistake in a light fish curry and a milestone in a rendang. What matters is whether you meant it.

Fixing a split sauce

If it has broken and you did not want it to, take the pan off the heat and stop stirring vigorously, which only makes it worse. A splash of cold water or a spoon of fresh santan whisked in gently will often bring it back.

A small amount of ground candlenut, or even a little cornflour slurry, will stabilise a sauce that keeps wanting to break. Neither is traditional in every dish, but both are better than serving something curdled.

If it will not come back, reduce it further and lean into it. A sauce that has fully separated and been cooked down often tastes better than a rescued one, even if it looks less tidy.

Storing it

Fresh keeps a day in the fridge, two at the outside, and freezes reasonably well if you are organised. Opened boxes and cans keep three or four days refrigerated, and both freeze; ice cube trays are the practical answer for the half-box you did not use.

Frozen and thawed santan often looks separated and slightly grainy. Whisk it and use it in cooking rather than in anything raw or delicate, and it will be fine.
