Reading a Malaysian food label

A fixed set of legally required information, and a much larger amount of marketing. Separating the two takes five minutes.

Section
Halal
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
7 min

A Malaysian food label carries a fixed set of legally required information and a much larger amount of marketing. Learning to separate the two takes about five minutes and saves a great deal of money.

What has to be there

Regardless of what the front of the pack says, the following are required: the product name, an ingredient list, net weight or volume, date marking, allergen information, and the name and address of the manufacturer, packer or importer. Labelling appears in Bahasa Malaysia, often alongside English.

Everything else is the manufacturer's choice, and that includes most of what the front of the pack is shouting at you.

The ingredient list is the useful part

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight. That single rule tells you more than any claim on the front.

If sugar appears second in a savoury sauce, it is a sweet product whatever the label calls it. If the thing pictured on the front appears sixth, there is very little of it in there. If the first three ingredients are water, sugar and starch, you know what you are buying before reading another word.

Read the first three. They are usually most of the product by weight, and they are usually the honest summary.

Two different dates

This trips people up constantly, and the two mean genuinely different things.

Baik sebelum, best before, is about quality. The product is expected to be at its best until that date. Afterwards it may be stale, softer, less aromatic; it is generally not dangerous. Dry goods, biscuits and many packaged staples carry this.

Tarikh luput, expiry or use by, is about safety. This is the one to respect, and it appears on things where deterioration is a genuine risk.

Treating the first as though it were the second is responsible for a lot of avoidable waste. Treating the second as though it were the first is the mistake that matters.

Words with no legal meaning

Natural. Premium. Traditional. Authentic. Homestyle. Original recipe. Chef's choice. None of these are defined or controlled, and any product may use them.

They are not lies exactly. They simply carry no information, and treating them as evidence of quality is how people end up paying more for the same thing in a nicer bag.

Who actually made it

The address panel distinguishes between manufacturer, packer, importer and distributor, and the wording is deliberate.

"Manufactured by" means what it says. "Packed by" or "Packed for" means someone else made it and this company put it in the bag. "Imported by" and "Distributed by" mean the company on the label did neither.

None of this is a problem, and contract manufacturing is entirely normal. But if you are trying to work out where something is genuinely produced, this panel is where the answer is, and it is often not the brand on the front.

The nutrition panel and its moving target

Nutrition information is typically given per 100g and per serving. The per-100g column is the one to use for comparisons, because it is fixed.

Serving sizes are chosen by the manufacturer, and they are chosen to flatter. A snack that reports modest sugar per serving may be declaring a serving as a third of the packet, which is not how anybody eats it. Two products can look very different per serving and be nearly identical per 100g.

If you compare only one number across products, make it per 100g.

Batch codes

The batch or lot code looks like noise, and it is the most useful thing on the pack if something goes wrong. It is how a recall is targeted, and how a manufacturer traces a complaint back to a specific production run.

For a business buying ingredients, recording batch codes on incoming goods is not bureaucracy for its own sake: it is what makes your own traceability work, and it is one of the first things an auditor asks to see.

What the label cannot tell you

A label declares what is in a product. It does not tell you where each ingredient came from, which supplier produced it, or whether a given emulsifier was derived from plants or animals.

That is not concealment; it is simply the limit of what labelling is designed to do. It is also why halal status cannot be reasoned out from an ingredient panel, however carefully you read it. We went through exactly which ingredients are ambiguous, and why, in the grey areas.

For that question, the certificate is the answer and the panel is not.
