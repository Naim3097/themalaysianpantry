Food business

The licences you actually need to sell food in Malaysia

Most people discover the requirements in the wrong order: they sign a tenancy first, then find out the unit will not pass inspection.

Section
Food business
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
9 min

Most people starting a food business in Malaysia discover the licensing requirements in the wrong order: they find a shop lot, sign a tenancy, and only then learn that the premises will not pass a council inspection. The sequence matters more than the paperwork.

This is the order things actually need to happen in, and what each step involves.

1. Register the business with SSM

Everything else depends on this. Suruhanjaya Syarikat Malaysia registration gives the business a legal identity, and every subsequent licence application will ask for the number. Sole proprietorship, partnership or Sdn Bhd are all fine; the choice affects tax and liability rather than food licensing.

Registration is done online through SSM's ezBiz portal and is the cheapest and fastest step in this entire list. Do it first.

2. Premises licence from your local council

The lesen premis comes from your local authority, the PBT: DBKL in Kuala Lumpur, MBSA in Shah Alam, MPK in Klang, and so on. This is the step that catches people out, for two reasons.

First, requirements vary by council. Two towns thirty minutes apart can want different documents and apply different standards. Whatever a guide tells you, including this one, check with your actual council.

Second, it involves a physical inspection of the premises. Grease traps, ventilation, washing facilities, pest control, the state of the floors and walls. If the unit is not suitable, you are either paying to renovate or looking for another unit, and either is far more expensive after you have signed a lease.

Fees generally sit in the low hundreds of ringgit a year and vary by council and business type.

Check the premises before you sign

Ask the council what they require for your business type before committing to a unit, and ideally walk the space with someone who has been through it. A shop lot that was previously a clothing store may need substantial work to satisfy a food inspection.

This single step, taken in the wrong order, is the most expensive mistake in this article.

3. Food handler training and typhoid vaccination

Mandatory for everyone who handles food or drink, and that includes you, not only your staff.

The training is a short course from an accredited provider, available online or in person, and costs around fifty ringgit a head. The anti-typhoid injection is separate, available at clinics, and needs repeating every three years.

Keep the certificates where you can produce them. Inspectors ask, and "we did it last year" is not an answer if the paperwork is at home.

4. FoSIM registration

The Food Safety Information System of Malaysia, run by the Ministry of Health. Registration here is what puts your operation into the national food safety system, and it is a prerequisite for the manufacturing certifications below.

5. MeSTI, if you are manufacturing

If you are producing packaged food rather than serving it directly, MeSTI is the baseline food safety certification, and there is a detail here worth knowing because it changes the calculation for small producers.

The Ministry of Health does not charge for MeSTI certification, and offers technical coaching alongside it at no cost. A great many small producers assume certification means consultant fees and postpone it for years. The certificate itself is free; what it costs you is the effort of meeting the requirements and keeping the records.

Beyond MeSTI, GMP and HACCP come into play when you start supplying supermarkets, hotel groups or export customers. We covered what separates those three in MESTI, GMP and HACCP in plain language.

Halal is separate, and optional

Halal certification is not part of the licensing sequence. It is a different authority, a different standard and a voluntary choice, however commercially important it may be for your market.

You can operate entirely legally without it. You cannot label or market a product as halal without it. If you intend to pursue it, the useful move is to start collecting supplier documentation now rather than later, for the reasons set out in how halal certification actually works.

If you are cooking from home

Home-based food businesses occupy genuinely uncertain ground, and the honest answer is that it depends on your council and what you are selling.

Some councils have a route for small home operations. Others take the position that a residential address cannot hold a food premises licence at all. Selling occasional kuih to neighbours and running a daily catering operation from the same kitchen are treated very differently, and the line between them is not always written down.

Anyone telling you confidently that home food businesses are simply legal or simply illegal in Malaysia is generalising past a lot of local variation. Ask your own council, and get the answer from them rather than from a forum.

The realistic sequence

  • Before signing anything — ask your council what your business type requires
  • Week one — SSM registration
  • Then — premises licence application and inspection
  • In parallel — food handler training and typhoid jabs for everyone
  • Then — FoSIM, and MeSTI if you are manufacturing
  • Later, if the market wants it — halal, then GMP or HACCP

None of this is difficult in isolation. What makes it painful is doing it out of order, and the most common way to do that is to fall in love with a premises before finding out what it would take to license it.
