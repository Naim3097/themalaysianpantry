Belacan: how it is made, how to use it, how to store it

The ingredient that makes a kitchen smell like something has gone badly wrong, right up until the moment it makes everything taste right.

Section
Ingredients
Published
07.09.2026
Reading
8 min

Every Malaysian kitchen has a block of it somewhere, usually triple-wrapped and pushed to the back of a cupboard like something being hidden from the authorities. Belacan is not a subtle ingredient. It is also the reason sambal belacan tastes like sambal belacan and not like chilli paste, and there is no way around it.

What follows is the practical stuff: what you are actually buying, how to tell a good block from a poor one, why toasting matters more than any other step, and how to store it so your rice does not absorb it.

What it actually is

Belacan is made from geragau, the tiny shrimp that come in enormous quantities off the coast. They are salted, left to ferment, dried in the sun, then pounded and pressed into blocks. That is the whole process, and it has not changed much in a very long time.

The fermentation is doing the work. It breaks the shrimp proteins down into free amino acids, which is the same thing happening in fish sauce, in aged cheese, in soy sauce. What you get back is glutamate, concentrated, in a form that dissolves into whatever you are cooking and makes it taste more like itself. This is why a spoonful of belacan in a dish that contains no other seafood still tastes deeper rather than fishy.

Penang and Melaka both have strong reputations for it, and people from each will tell you theirs is better. Malacca belacan tends to be firmer and darker. Penang blocks are often a touch softer. Both are fine. This is not a fight worth having.

Judging a block at the market

You are looking at three things, and you can assess all of them without opening the packet.

Colour

Good belacan runs from a deep pinkish-brown to a proper dark brown-purple. Very pale blocks usually mean more filler and less shrimp. Very dark, almost black blocks are not automatically bad, but they are often older.

Firmness

It should be dense and hold together, not crumble when pressed through the wrapper. A block that feels damp or soft has more moisture in it than you want, and moisture is what turns belacan sour in storage rather than merely pungent.

The smell

Sharp, salty, marine, strong. What you do not want is anything ammoniac. That edge means it has gone past fermented into spoiled, and toasting will not rescue it.

On price

Belacan is cheap even at the good end, and the difference between a mediocre block and an excellent one is usually a couple of ringgit. This is one of the few ingredients where buying the better version costs almost nothing and changes the dish noticeably. Buy the better one.

Toasting is not optional

Raw belacan is harsh. It tastes flatly salty and smells much worse than it needs to. Toasting drives off moisture, mellows the aggression and brings out the roasted, nutty depth that the ingredient is actually valued for. Skipping it is the single most common mistake, and it is the reason some people conclude they do not like belacan.

Three methods, in order of how much you care about your kitchen.

Foil parcel, dry pan. Wrap the piece in foil, flatten it slightly, and toast in a dry pan over medium heat for a couple of minutes per side. This is the standard approach and it contains most of the smell.

Direct in the pan. Crumble it into a dry pan and stir until it darkens and turns crumbly and aromatic. Faster, better flavour, and your house will smell like it for a while. Open a window before, not after.

Over a flame on a skewer. The traditional method, and the best for aroma. Also the most confrontational.

You are toasting until it is dry, crumbly and smells toasted rather than raw. Two to three minutes, usually. It goes bitter if you push it, so stop when the smell changes rather than waiting for a colour you have imagined.

How much to use

Less than you think. A piece the size of a thumbnail is enough for a sambal for four people. Belacan is a seasoning, not a base, and it is one of those ingredients where too much does not read as "more flavour" but as "something is wrong with this."

If a dish tastes muddy and slightly bitter and you cannot work out why, belacan is usually the answer.

Where it belongs

Sambal belacan most obviously, where it is pounded with chilli, a little salt and lime. Into a rempah for asam laksa, curries and most fried rice worth eating. Kangkung belacan. Rojak sauce, where it sits underneath the sweetness and stops the whole thing being cloying. Nasi goreng, where a small amount transforms a plate that would otherwise taste of soy sauce and hope.

It also works quietly in places people do not expect. A trace in a marinade for grilled chicken adds savoury depth without announcing itself, particularly alongside sugar and lime.

Storage, and the smell problem

Belacan will scent anything porous within reach, and rice is the classic casualty. The method that works:

  • Wrap the block tightly in cling film or foil, pressing out air
  • Put that inside an airtight container, ideally glass
  • Keep it in a cool cupboard, or the fridge if your kitchen runs hot
  • Do not store it next to rice, flour, tea, coffee or anything dried

Kept dry and sealed it lasts a very long time, well over a year. What ruins it is moisture, not age. A block that has been left half-wrapped in a humid kitchen will sour long before an old one that has stayed sealed.

Some people freeze it. It works and it does no harm, though it is rarely necessary in a dry container.

Substitutes, honestly

There is no true substitute, and articles that claim otherwise are usually written by people who have not cooked with it.

Thai kapi is the closest relative and will get you to a similar place in a curry paste, though it is wetter, saltier and less roasted. Fish sauce provides savoury depth but none of the toasted body, so a dish made with fish sauce instead of belacan tastes thinner and sharper. Dried shrimp adds shrimp flavour without the fermented complexity.

If you are cooking for someone who genuinely cannot eat shellfish, the honest advice is to cook something else rather than to approximate it. Sambal belacan without belacan is just sambal, and that is a perfectly good thing to make on purpose.

One last thing

If you have only ever had belacan in a dish that was heavy-handed with it, you have not really had it. The good version is not loud. It sits underneath everything and makes the chilli taste more like chilli and the lime taste sharper. You notice its absence more than its presence, which is the mark of most ingredients worth understanding properly.
